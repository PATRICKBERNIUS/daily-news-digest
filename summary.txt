Good morning. Let's start with the biggest story in AI right now, which is actually about money and markets rather than technology itself. There are growing signs of a "reverse AI trade" playing out globally. Market expert Ajay Bagga is pointing to foreign investors pulling back from AI infrastructure bets in the US, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, and redirecting that money into Indian equities instead, drawn by more attractive valuations and falling crude prices. The catch is that rising US inflation could complicate this shift, so it's one to watch rather than a done deal. This rotation comes against the backdrop of a much bigger AI story: Anthropic, the maker of Claude, is pursuing an IPO that could raise as much as 64 billion dollars and value the company at up to 1.4 trillion dollars. That's an enormous bet, and it's effectively a referendum on whether Wall Street still believes in the AI boom at these valuations, especially as capital starts rotating elsewhere.

There's also a geopolitical wrinkle brewing around Nvidia. Allegations that a woman connected to CEO Jensen Huang was a Chinese spy could trigger tighter US enforcement of AI chip export restrictions, according to analysts. The ripple effects would be felt most in Southeast Asia, where cloud computing providers may face more scrutiny, and the controversy could open doors for rival chipmakers looking to grab market share while Nvidia navigates the fallout.

On the corporate AI front, OpenAI continues its acquisition spree, picking up the AI travel planning startup Odessia less than a year after it launched, with founder Francis Davidson joining the team to help build out travel features. Meanwhile, Adobe and Google are deepening their partnership, with Google's AI models getting integrated further into Adobe's creative suite, including Photoshop, Premiere, Acrobat and Adobe Express. And in the Gulf, Dubai's banking sector is accelerating its AI adoption too, with major lenders like Emirates NBD moving past experimentation and starting to treat AI as a core strategic priority for how they compete, according to discussions at last week's Dubai FinTech Summit.

Closer to the ground, there's a recurring theme of AI accessibility for smaller players. In India, there's a push to make cloud infrastructure and AI tools more affordable for micro, small and medium enterprises, which otherwise can't match the spending power of large companies. In a similar vein, agri-fintech is looking at how technology, like digital warehousing and a proposed "farm score," could help farmers access loans against stored produce instead of being forced into distress sales during harvest season.

On the hardware and connectivity side, Fibocom showcased a new smart module blending computing and connectivity for smarter AIoT devices, part of a broader trend of devices evolving from simple connectivity terminals into more intelligent, interactive systems. Separately, thermal imaging continues to expand beyond security and industrial use into automotive and consumer electronics, with a new market report showing the top five manufacturers, led by China's Raytron, now controlling about 90 percent of global shipments. And in the UK, BT has paused its ambitious plan to use Starlink technology to boost rural broadband, with the CEO saying they want to wait until the controversy around Elon Musk's Starlink, including its role in disrupting Ukraine's military operations, settles down.

Switching to biotech and health, there's a genuinely exciting development: scientists have managed to regrow damaged heart muscle for the first time, a breakthrough that could offer real hope to the roughly one million people in the UK alone living with heart failure from weakened or stiffened heart muscle. It's early, but it's being called a major milestone for heart attack recovery. In the biotech business world, Prime Medicine priced a public stock offering expected to bring in about 125 million dollars to fund its gene-editing therapies, while Immutep expanded its US patent portfolio for combination cancer and infection immunotherapies built around its efti platform. In India, the government highlighted a "biotechnology-driven industrial revolution" at the Biotechnology Research and Innovation Council's foundation day, signaling continued policy focus on the sector. And in Singapore, Sunmax Biotechnology marked 25 years in collagen innovation with a regional summit bringing together aesthetic medicine experts from across Asia-Pacific.

Finally, a few business and markets notes worth knowing: India's deal-making activity hit 12 billion dollars in October, up 8 percent month-on-month, boosted significantly by the Fairfax-IDBI Bank acquisition. Kissht's parent company OnEMI raised 832 crore rupees in a preferential share issue backed by Axis Mutual Fund, Citigroup and MIT, with its stock up 70 percent over six months. UK fintech Tide announced a 250 million pound investment in India over the next four years to build out tech centers, even as it exits its direct member-facing business there, affecting around 400 jobs. And Standard Engineering Technology, which serves pharma, biotech and AI data center clients, posted strong Q2 results with revenue up 62 percent and net profit up 38 percent.
