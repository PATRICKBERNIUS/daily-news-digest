Good morning. Let's start with what's dominating markets today, because it all comes back to one theme: nervousness about whether the AI boom is overbuilt. Asian stocks slumped again on renewed AI worry, dragging futures lower for the FTSE 100 ahead of a big day of corporate earnings, and oil prices actually rose as Middle East tensions added another layer of concern about energy supplies just as technology companies keep pouring money into AI infrastructure. A small Korean AI cloud company called Elice became an unlikely symbol of this anxiety this week, with its shares plunging 32 percent on its stock market debut despite strong institutional demand, then roaring back nearly 30 percent — a wild swing that traders are now reading as a litmus test for how fragile sentiment around AI stocks really is. Underscoring just how central AI has become to the global economy, a survey of energy executives found that more than 80 percent believe current forecasts are underestimating how much electricity AI will actually demand, raising real questions about whether global power infrastructure can keep up with data center growth. That tension is playing out on the ground in the US too, where tech giants building massive data centers are facing growing pushback from host communities, and are now offering to take fewer tax breaks, share more revenue, and be more transparent in hopes of smoothing over local opposition. Nokia, meanwhile, is riding the other side of this wave, reporting continued strong growth in AI and data-center business as telecom infrastructure demand stays robust.

On the corporate and investment side, AI is reshaping deal-making far beyond Silicon Valley. In India, private equity investors are now using AI tools to analyze target companies, assess competitive positioning, and even gain leverage in negotiations by quickly spotting a business's weaknesses. A major Gulf-Europe deal is also in the works, with Mubadala and Greece's sovereign wealth fund signing a renewed three-year partnership alongside roughly 15 memorandums of understanding covering energy, AI, exports and healthcare, plus a potential $10 billion Greek-Emirati construction joint venture. In retail, Walmart just unveiled tools letting AI shopping agents like Meta's Muse shop on behalf of customers — a notably different stance from Amazon, which still blocks outside AI agents from its platform. Speaking of Amazon, it's pledging to create 30,000 entry-level jobs for young people in the UK by 2030, part of a broader wave of corporate hiring commitments that includes Hyatt Hotels, which plans to add over 30,000 jobs and expand from 53 to more than 250 properties in India by the same year, with AI and technology training built into that growth. There's also a steady drumbeat of AI education and workforce stories: GITAM University in India is rolling out AI workshops aiming to reach over 3,000 schools, and there's a broader push in leadership circles for executives to rethink how they manage in an AI-driven world, where decision-making is faster and judgment matters more, not less.

A couple of smaller business stories worth flagging: analysts are already dissecting how Elon Musk's floated merger of Tesla and SpaceX would navigate serious regulatory hurdles, especially around Tesla's deep manufacturing ties to China and SpaceX's sensitive defense contracts — a firewall around Tesla's China operations is one proposed fix, though divestment would be messy given how embedded Tesla is with local suppliers there. And RBC has updated its earnings estimates for insurer Allianz, flagging both weather-related catastrophe losses and AI as factors to watch heading into its third-quarter results.

Turning to biotech and health, there's a lot of forward motion on new therapies. INmune Bio is advancing its cell therapy Ebstrocel for a rare skin disease called recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, having just submitted its final pre-approval package to UK regulators, with a full marketing application targeted for early 2027 and a US application expected not long after; the company also just struck a distribution deal with Uniphar to set up global commercialization once it's approved. Unicycive Therapeutics got a win too, with the FDA accepting its resubmitted application for a kidney disease drug called oxylanthanum carbonate, setting a target decision date of March 2027. In vaccine news, Patronus Biotech received ethical approval in Australia to begin first-in-human trials of a new malaria vaccine candidate targeting both major malaria-causing parasites. And there's an interesting piece on the push for fridge-free vaccines — building on a UK trial of a heat-stable tetanus-diphtheria vaccine — which could be a game-changer for immunization access in parts of Africa where cold-chain infrastructure is unreliable, though experts caution there are still real practical hurdles to work through. On the corporate leadership front, Australian biotech giant CSL named GSK executive Deborah Waterhouse as its next CEO, making her the first woman to lead the company in its three-decade history. And ScionxBio announced a global licensing deal with Sanofi to develop longer-lasting mRNA therapeutics using its proprietary platform, a sign that next-generation mRNA technology continues to attract serious pharma partnerships.

Finally, on the education and workforce side of biotech, IIT Madras launched a new "finishing school" called UDYOG aimed at giving biotechnology and biomanufacturing graduates practical, industry-ready skills — part of a broader effort to close the gap between academic training and what biotech employers actually need.

That's the briefing — AI anxiety and AI opportunity both running hot in the markets, major hiring and infrastructure commitments from big employers, and a steady stream of biotech progress from rare disease therapies to malaria vaccines and next-gen mRNA deals.
