Good morning. Let's start with the story dominating tech circles today: Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has resigned, and it's a messy exit. His departure Thursday follows reports of extramarital affairs, and separately, claims have surfaced that he was the target of a Chinese espionage plot. Beijing has pushed back hard on that angle, dismissing the spying allegations as "groundless speculation" and accusing the US of using them as a pretext to justify a broader technological blockade against China. Either way, Nvidia — the company that's essentially powered the entire AI boom — now has to figure out its future without the man who built it, and that's a genuinely uncertain moment given how central Nvidia's chips are to everything happening in AI right now.

Speaking of AI's scale, Goldman Sachs dropped a striking forecast: they expect AI-related spending on hardware and intellectual property to hit $1.4 trillion annualized by the end of 2027, which would be nearly 4% of the entire US economy. They're calling AI the dominant force behind business investment for the next couple of years, with spending topping $900 billion by the end of 2026 alone. That's a big number, and it underscores just how much of the economy is now betting on this technology paying off.

On the corporate leadership side, Sundar Pichai has topped the HSBC Hurun Global Indians List for 2026, with Alphabet's valuation hitting $4.2 trillion and now overtaking Microsoft. It's part of a broader trend — the list features 236 global leaders of Indian descent whose companies are collectively worth $15 trillion, and notably includes 18 AI companies worth $227 billion, showing how much Indian-origin leadership is shaping this industry. Meanwhile, in a smaller but telling story about how even tech executives are using AI personally, Mark Zuckerberg says he's building an AI agent to help him with his CEO duties, but when he talks about his personal AI agent, he's using it less for business and more to help him be a better father and husband — a pretty human detail from one of the industry's biggest names.

Not everyone's cheering the AI agent trend, though. JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon is warning consumers and financial institutions not to let AI agents handle things like making payments on your behalf, despite developers boasting about what these autonomous agents can do — booking travel, running errands, executing purchases. He's essentially urging caution before people hand over that level of control. On the safety front, OpenAI has introduced something called "Control Room," a new system designed to shut down a rogue or unexpectedly behaving AI model within 35 seconds, giving researchers more direct ability to intervene if a model starts posing security risks.

There's also a quieter story about AI and authenticity: the Caine Prize for African Writing has cleared Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza of allegations that he used AI to write his award-winning story, but the prize is also rolling out new rules to guard against generative AI in future submissions — a sign that literary and creative institutions are still working out how to police this.

On the consumer tech side, Google's November Pixel Drop is bringing Gemini AI features, including smart writing tools, to older Pixel phones, extending the life of devices that aren't brand new. And if you're a Samsung watcher, leaks are circulating about the Galaxy S27 Pro, expected to have a 6.6-inch display, a 200MP camera, a Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 6 Extreme chipset, and a 5,200mAh battery, with a launch expected in India early next year.

There's also a notable IP dispute brewing in the auto world: Jaguar Land Rover has taken Chinese EV maker BYD to court in the Netherlands, accusing BYD's Fangchengbao Ti7 SUV of ripping off the Defender's design and technology. Dutch authorities have already raided and seized documents from BYD's offices, with a court decision expected this week — worth watching given how often Chinese manufacturers have faced these kinds of accusations.

Now to biotech and health, where there's a lot happening. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei made a bold claim that cancer could be "history" within ten years, as his company ramps up efforts to use AI to accelerate drug discovery in partnership with biotech firms. In India, a Hyderabad startup called PopVax is now offering personalized mRNA cancer vaccines — built around the specific genetic mutations in an individual patient's tumor — through its RNA Foundry, though the price tag is steep at around $3 million, and there are still open questions about evidence, billing transparency, and regulatory approval. On a similar note, Denmark's Commit Biologics has nominated its first clinical development candidate, COM002, from its complement-engaging platform, and made two senior leadership hires as it moves from early-stage research toward Phase 1 trials expected to dose its first patient in 2028. In the UK, T-Cypher Bio is expanding its lab space in Oxford as it continues developing T-cell engager therapies targeting cancer antigens.

There's also a fascinating science piece today on the dream of de-extinction — using ancient DNA sequencing, genome analysis, and gene editing to potentially bring back extinct species. Scientists are pushing back on how realistic that really is, offering a useful reality check on a topic that gets a lot of hype.

On the policy and investment side, India's CII president is encouraging industry to use free trade agreements to build joint ventures and attract investment, specifically flagging AI and biotechnology as sectors that stand to benefit. Karnataka's government is also proposing a ₹50-crore bio-economy mission for the Kalyana Karnataka region, aiming to use local biological resources and modern biotech to boost agriculture and local manufacturing.

Finally, a few business and market notes: Hindustan Aeronautics posted a 16% jump in quarterly profit, though margins are under some pressure. Standard Engineering Technology, which serves pharma, biotech, and AI data center industries, reported a strong 62% revenue jump for the quarter. And if you're investing in health-adjacent biotech, there's commentary floating around today favoring Telix Pharmaceuticals over CSL, given CSL's recent volatility and write-downs. That's the roundup — plenty of movement in AI investment and leadership, and some genuinely exciting, if early, developments in cancer treatment and biotech research.
