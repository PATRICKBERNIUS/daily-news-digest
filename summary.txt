Good morning. Let's start with the story dominating tech circles today: the fallout from Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia CEO. After 33 years at the helm, Huang stepped down amid controversies tied to his personal life, including reports that an associate, Alice Huang, allegedly helped facilitate Chinese companies' access to billions of dollars worth of restricted Nvidia chips. The timing is almost cinematic: the same day Huang resigned, TSMC confirmed a data breach exposing current and former employee information, and Microsoft's Outlook suffered a global outage. Analysts are already weighing in on the ripple effects, particularly for China. With Huang gone, Chinese AI firms lose their most influential advocate in Washington, someone who regularly pushed back against export restrictions and championed distillation and open-source models that disproportionately benefited Chinese developers. Without that voice, expect louder calls for tighter controls on AI exports to China.

Meanwhile, Oaktree's Howard Marks is injecting some caution into the broader AI enthusiasm. In a widely circulated Q&A, he acknowledges AI could be one of the most consequential technologies in history, but he's flagging a real disconnect between the technology's success and whether investors actually profit from it. His worry is that if AI demand, chip economics, or corporate spending disappoint, the damage won't stay contained to tech stocks, it could bleed into credit markets, construction, and broader earnings across the economy. It's a useful counterweight to the breathless investment narrative dominating headlines lately.

On the education and workforce side, there's a real tension emerging around AI access and literacy. A RAND study found that the gap between wealthy and low-income families in how their teens use AI for schoolwork has widened from 15 to 21 percentage points, raising concerns about a new kind of digital divide. At the same time, voices in the workforce are cautioning that surviving the AI era will depend less on technical mastery and more on holding onto basic human skills like communication. South Korea, meanwhile, is taking a more defensive posture, with lawmakers requesting nearly five million dollars to detect AI-powered smart glasses being used to cheat on exams, a problem that's spread from college entrance tests to professional licensing exams. And in India, Meta is rolling out its AI-based age-detection system globally this month, aiming to catch teens who lie about their age to bypass Instagram's protections, automatically shifting suspected minors into restricted Teen Accounts.

AI is also seeping into politics and everyday skepticism. In India, Samajwadi Party chief Akhilesh Yadav waded into the controversy over Rahul Gandhi's phone-tapping allegations against the Modi government, arguing that no phone or modern technology can really be trusted anymore in the AI era. And in a smaller but telling sign of AI's cultural penetration, a viral claim that adding a comma to your password protects you from data breaches spread partly because people were asking Grok, X's AI chatbot, to validate it, prompting a wider fact-check debate.

On the education and business front, a Tiruchirappalli-based management school, BIM, has partnered with the University of Texas at Arlington to launch a joint Master's program in AI for Business, reflecting how fast institutions are moving to formalize AI skills training.

Switching to biotech, there's a lot of clinical momentum today. Pasithea Therapeutics presented strong Phase 1/1b data for its drug PAS-004, aimed at neurofibromatosis type 1 patients. Every evaluable participant with cutaneous neurofibromas saw tumor reductions, with a median shrinkage of 41 percent, and the benefits deepened over time, reaching 71 percent reduction in some patients by month fifteen. The safety profile looked clean too, with no serious treatment-related adverse events, and the FDA has already cleared the path for a Phase 2 trial. In the cardiovascular space, Cyclarity Therapeutics got its own FDA green light, an IND clearance for UDP-003, a cyclodextrin-based therapy designed using an AI-enabled computational platform to reverse atherosclerosis by extracting oxidized cholesterol from cells.

There's also notable capital-markets activity in biotech. Windward Bio, a Basel-based company developing long-acting antibody treatments for respiratory and skin diseases, priced its IPO at seventeen dollars a share, expecting to raise around 225 million dollars. San Diego-based Axiom Biosciences took a different route, opening a crowdfunding round through Wefunder to give retail investors access to its regenerative therapy pipeline. And in France, HTL Biotechnology inaugurated a new fifteen-million-euro production facility dedicated to pharmaceutical-grade DNA polynucleotides, part of a larger hundred-million-euro investment plan aimed at meeting growing global demand for regenerative medicine inputs, especially from Asian markets.

Rounding out biotech, there's a sustainability angle too. A 25-year-old IIT Delhi biotech graduate has drawn attention for her startup Yotuh Energy, which is tackling food waste by reimagining cold-chain transport without relying on truck engines. And in Karnataka, officials are proposing a fifty-crore rupee "bio-economy mission" to help farmers add value to local crops like tur dal and the Kamalapur red banana through biotechnology, aiming to boost rural incomes across seven districts.

That's the roundup for today, plenty of AI anxiety and opportunity colliding, and biotech continuing to rack up clinical wins and fresh capital even as the broader market tries to figure out who actually profits from all this innovation.
