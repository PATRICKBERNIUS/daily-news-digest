Good morning. Let's start with artificial intelligence, since there's a lot moving there today. Bill Gates has stirred up conversation with a warning that AI could trigger two decades of economic and social turmoil, saying disruption to jobs and daily life is likely even if the technology is managed responsibly. That warning dovetails with news out of India, where Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal says the government is actively identifying "AI-proof" professions, particularly in traditional sectors where India's skilled workforce has an edge and where technology can boost efficiency without simply replacing people. On a more philosophical note, lyricist Javed Akhtar weighed in at the Mumbai LitFest, arguing that AI may never truly master poetry because it can't experience grief, heartbreak or rejection firsthand, the very things that give human poets their emotional depth.

On the corporate and product side, Jack Dorsey's Block has unveiled Dessa, a new AI voice model notable for being trained entirely on human conversation rather than text or pretrained models, a different approach from how most voice AI has been built. Meanwhile, Chinese AI firm Moonshot AI has launched Kimi K3.1 Pro, a massive 3-trillion-parameter open-weight model with a million-token context window, plus a faster variant, and the company says it plans to release the full model weights by November, a notable move toward openness as competition with Western labs intensifies. OpenAI, meanwhile, is tightening up on security, announcing that its most advanced models will now be trained completely offline, without access to the live internet, citing concerns about current safety limitations.

There's also a geopolitical thread running through the tech world right now. Tensions around H-1B visas are boiling over in the US, with growing hostility toward Indian and Indian-American tech workers becoming a flashpoint that's rattling Silicon Valley hiring, housing markets, and local economies that depend on global talent. On the diplomatic and investment front, Scotland's First Minister John Swinney visited Gujarat Biotechnology University to discuss academic partnerships, Karnataka's IT minister pitched the state as a gateway for Polish investment during a meeting with Poland's prime minister, and a wave of new memoranda between Greece and the UAE could unlock up to €12 billion in investment spanning AI, biotechnology, defense and energy.

Turning to biotech, it's a busy day for funding and deals. Startup financing news shows at least $790 million flowing into sectors like space infrastructure, defense biotech, industrial AI and robotics in just the last day, with California's Xona Space Systems leading the pack at a $250 million raise. One of the more eyebrow-raising stories involves Warhound, a startup co-founded by Anduril's Palmer Luckey and Colossal Biosciences' Ben Lamm, which just raised $200 million to develop genetically enhanced military working dogs for the US, blending de-extinction biotech with defense technology. In more traditional biotech news, Congruence Therapeutics presented promising early data on a new oral treatment for Alpha-1 Antitrypsin deficiency, showing it increased functional protein levels in lab and mouse models. Ping An Biomedical announced plans to acquire three cancer-related medical technology patents to build out its oncology IP portfolio. And on the education front, University of Maine at Farmington students can now start earning credit toward a biotechnology master's degree from Northeastern while still finishing their undergraduate work, a sign of growing investment in training the next generation of biotech talent.

In politics touching biotech, Donald Trump publicly defended Vivek Ramaswamy, the biotech entrepreneur turned Ohio gubernatorial candidate, after backlash over a campaign ad targeting his rival Amy Acton's family.

A couple of lighter but interesting items to round things out. HPE gave a look inside its Technology Renewal Centre near Glasgow, where old laptops and printers are refurbished and given a second life rather than scrapped, a small window into the growing push for tech sustainability. And in space science, Chinese astronomers have discovered a real three-star system that, unlike the chaotic worlds imagined in Liu Cixin's science fiction, actually appears stable, a fascinating real-world counterpoint to "The Three-Body Problem."

That's the roundup for today, a lot of movement in AI policy and security, big money flowing into biotech and defense-adjacent startups, and some genuinely strange science and tech stories mixed in.
