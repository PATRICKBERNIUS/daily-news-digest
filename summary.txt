Good morning. Let's start with artificial intelligence, which continues to dominate the headlines in both promising and concerning ways. On the encouraging side, there's a pilot project in Kuala Lumpur at Razak Mansion using AI, radar, and thermal sensors to detect falls and unusual inactivity among elderly residents living alone, essentially giving them an invisible safety net. In India, a similar spirit of using AI for good is showing up through the "Adi Vaani" platform, which is helping bridge the digital divide for tribal communities by connecting them to online services and information. And in healthcare, there's a genuinely exciting development out of Manas AI, where researchers say artificial intelligence is slashing early-stage cancer drug discovery time from nearly eight years down to just three or four months by using machine learning combined with physics and chemistry principles to identify promising compounds instead of testing them one by one.

But alongside these bright spots, there's growing unease about AI's darker applications. In South Korea, police are investigating the top scorer of a national tax accountant exam after he admitted to using AI to cheat, raising fresh questions about academic integrity. In Malaysia, politician Fadillah Yusof is warning campaign workers ahead of Sarawak's state election to be on guard against AI-generated fake news, deepfakes, and manipulated videos. And at the ASEAN Business and Investment Summit, Nobel laureate Maria Ressa delivered a stark warning that AI can amplify "the worst of who we are," cautioning that private sector deployment of the technology has far outpaced government regulation. Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim echoed similar concerns, urging ASEAN nations not to let a handful of global tech giants dictate the future of AI development and governance given its growing influence over economies and national security.

Regulators are responding. In India, the Securities and Exchange Board, Sebi, is preparing detailed guidelines for responsible AI use in capital markets, aiming to balance innovation with investor protection and accountability, alongside a new working group to support startup growth. Meanwhile, Goldman Sachs projects the global economy will grow 2.8 percent in 2027, buoyed partly by continued AI investment and falling oil prices, even as geopolitical tensions and fiscal pressures loom. Tied to that same economic outlook, Goldman also forecasts India will outpace China with 7.2 percent growth by 2027, positioning it as a major global economic force.

On the broader technology front, India's Defence Minister Rajnath Singh is pushing the country to shift from being a technology consumer to a global innovator, particularly in defense-related fields like non-destructive testing. In infrastructure news, Pune's metropolitan development authority is rolling out a GIS-based app using GPS and drone technology to crack down on illegal construction and monitor fire safety compliance. Over in the UK, Formula E's ultra-fast charging technology, think 600 kilowatt batteries, could make its way to everyday roads by 2030. And on a cautionary note, a deep-dive piece highlights how America's aviation system is still relying on floppy disks and aging computers, warning that the next major disruption could stem from something as small as a router failure. Speaking of aviation hiccups, an IndiGo flight from Pune to Guwahati had to turn back Sunday after a landing gear malfunction, though all passengers were safely evacuated.

In biotechnology, it's been a notably strong day for the sector. Windward Bio, a clinical-stage immunology biotech backed by Qiming Venture Partners, made a splashy Nasdaq debut, with shares jumping 68 percent on its first trading day and pushing the company's valuation to roughly 1.7 billion dollars. It's part of a broader wave of biotech IPOs from Qiming this year. In gene therapy news, Andelyn Biosciences has partnered with South Korea's Celliaz to advance an AAV-based gene therapy targeting retinitis pigmentosa, a step forward for treating inherited retinal degeneration. And there's a nice human interest story too: Venkat Nelabhotla, who leads Nasdaq-listed Vyome Holdings, shared his journey from a Telugu-medium government school to leading a biotech company, a reminder that career paths in science and entrepreneurship can start from humble beginnings.

That rounds out the major threads for today, AI's dual role as both a safety and innovation tool and a source of new risks, continued momentum in biotech dealmaking and gene therapy, and a mixed bag of technology stories spanning aviation safety, infrastructure monitoring, and economic forecasting.
