Good morning. Let's start with artificial intelligence, because it's dominating nearly every corner of today's news.

On the money side, AI investment is still roaring. A fresh batch of startup funding news shows billions flowing into companies building AI applications and infrastructure, headlined by Sierra's massive $1.75 billion Series F at a $30 billion valuation. But the bigger theme across these deals is that the cost of running AI is becoming just as important as what it can do, meaning investors are now chasing efficiency plays, not just flashy capabilities. That tension between AI hype and financial reality showed up elsewhere too. Anthropic, fresh off a record-breaking $93.5 billion Nasdaq debut that outpaced even SpaceX's historic raise, saw its stock slip below its $83 IPO price in premarket trading, as rising interest rates stir concerns about whether AI valuations have gotten ahead of themselves. Fittingly, Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh is grappling with that exact question at a policy level, calling AI a "hinge" of history for U.S. economic leadership while also warning that the massive investment boom could be fueling inflationary pressure the Fed will need to navigate carefully.

There's also a growing undercurrent of unease about where AI is headed. Meta's chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, warned publicly that humanity risks losing control of AI as it advances toward superintelligence, calling the implications "enormous." That echoes a running theme from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, who has spent the past year and a half warning AI could wipe out half of entry-level white-collar jobs — and it's just emerged that he once floated the idea of keeping jobless, angry workers calm with games, a detail that's raising eyebrows given how seriously he's pushed the job-loss narrative. On a related note, a piece on AI agents digs into the governance question underpinning all of this: as organizations hand AI agents real authority to act rather than just advise, nobody has fully settled who's accountable when something goes wrong.

Regulators are starting to catch up to that exact problem. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority issued fresh guidance warning tech companies that AI agents offering financial services — like Meta's new "Muse" assistant — can trigger full financial regulation, regardless of whether a company calls itself just a tech provider. Meanwhile in India, the Supreme Court released its first draft regulations for AI use in courts, addressing how AI can assist with translating judgments or recording testimony while making clear human oversight remains essential in judicial decisions. And there's a sobering infrastructure angle too: a letter-to-the-editor is calling out the FAA for ignoring repeated warnings that rushed AI and hardware upgrades to the air traffic control system could be risky — a reminder that AI rollout isn't just a boardroom story.

On the applied side, AI is showing up in some interesting places. A geospatial AI startup incubated at IIT Delhi used satellite analysis to detect fresh construction at China's massive Yarlung Tsangpo dam project. In consumer tech, a founder behind the startup Coframe argues AI has made apps clunkier and more generic — what he calls the "enshittification" of interfaces — but believes the same technology can fix that by building personalized interfaces for each user. And Malaysia is leaning on AI for inclusion rather than just innovation, with its Digital Ministry pushing digital literacy into rural communities as part of a 2030 "AI Nation" strategy.

In health and biotech, the standout story is a UK trial that will be the world's largest AI breast cancer screening study, testing whether AI can help radiologists catch suspicious scans faster across 660,000 women without compromising safety. Separately, a Denmark-based biotech, HERVolution, released preclinical data showing its immunotherapy — targeting a type of ancient viral DNA embedded in our genome — improved insulin sensitivity and glucose control in early studies, hinting at broader healthspan benefits beyond its original cancer focus.

On the corporate biotech front, it was a rougher day for a couple of companies. Capricor Therapeutics shares plunged after the FDA rejected its Duchenne muscular dystrophy cell therapy, deramiocel, for a second time; the company has rescheduled a call to update investors and says it remains committed to pursuing approval. Kazia Therapeutics, meanwhile, pushed back a virtual event on its cancer drug NDL2 to early 2027 while it files additional patents. On a more upbeat note, Taiwan's Jyong Biotech signed a letter of intent with a Philippine healthcare company to explore commercializing one of its plant-derived drugs in that market.

There's also a steady drumbeat of biotech infrastructure news: Uttar Pradesh is positioning Pilibhit as a new pharma and biotech manufacturing hub, Madhya Pradesh has approved a 100-acre, Rs 237 crore deep-tech park in Ujjain where startups can rent lab space by the hour to build AI, drone, and semiconductor prototypes, and Politecnico di Milano opened its academic year spotlighting 174 "One Health" research projects done with external partners. Add to that a piece noting biotechnology is increasingly seen as a strong career path post-pandemic, and the overall picture is one of steady institutional investment in biotech capacity across multiple countries.

A few smaller items rounding out the day: Maeving unveiled electric motorcycles that can now charge to 80 percent in just an hour, a Pune committee proposed tough new penalties — including potential jail time — for exam paper leaks, and a NIT Jamshedpur researcher won a young scientist award for satellite-based agricultural technology work. And for something completely different, there's a viral claim making the rounds about new evidence supposedly supporting the resurrection story in the Bible — more of a curiosity than hard science, but it's generating buzz today.

That's the rundown — the throughline today is that AI's economic and governance questions are maturing fast, even as the technology itself keeps pushing into healthcare, courts, and everyday apps.
