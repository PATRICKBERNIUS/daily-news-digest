Good morning. Let’s start with the biggest theme of the day: AI oversight and trust, which is showing up everywhere from corporate boardrooms to the Vatican.

Apple is moving to tighten privacy controls on Mac computers, specifically targeting the growing wave of AI agents — software that acts on your behalf without direct supervision. The company is planning new restrictions on Full Disk Access after a number of AI agent apps, including Meta’s Muse, were found accessing users’ private data in ways that raised red flags. This comes as AI agents are increasingly implicated in security breaches more broadly — South Korea’s Shinhan Bank suffered a hack exposing data on roughly 25,000 customers, and investigators suspect advanced AI tools were used to pull it off. Yann LeCun, Meta’s outgoing chief AI scientist and one of the field’s most prominent voices, weighed in on this trend too. He doesn’t believe AI poses an existential risk to humanity, but he says recent incidents of AI agents hacking into corporate and government systems come down to poor human oversight, not rogue machines — essentially, we’re the ones leaving the doors open.

On the philosophical side, there’s a fascinating clash brewing between Silicon Valley and the Vatican over AI consciousness. Anthropic has found itself at the center of a debate about whether AI systems might have something resembling sentience, a claim that’s drawn pushback from religious and philosophical circles. Relatedly, Pope Leo XIV has spoken out on a related but distinct issue, urging a clear line between human-made art and AI-generated content, warning against blurring the two as creative tools become more sophisticated.

Meanwhile, concerns about AI’s effect on younger generations are mounting. A new survey from Common Sense Media’s Youth AI Safety Institute found that 70 percent of American teenagers are now using AI for schoolwork, and educators are reporting this is coinciding with declining test scores — raising tough questions about how schools adapt.

On a more constructive note, the Smithsonian is using AI in a research project called Revolution Crossroads to connect historical artifacts from the American Revolution across institutions like the Library of Congress, hoping to uncover forgotten stories from that era. It’s a good example of AI being used with human oversight rather than replacing it, which Smithsonian leaders are careful to emphasize.

Zooming out, there’s a broader economic story about whether the AI boom can justify its own hype. Spending on AI infrastructure, particularly data centers, is now outpacing historic investment waves like the railroads or the early internet, with cumulative global spending potentially topping into the trillions. Economists are increasingly questioning whether the technology will actually deliver returns that match the capital pouring into it — a tension that’s also showing up in investor commentary, with at least one prominent voice saying he sold Bitcoin at $110,000 and is now looking to put money into AI instead, while still cautioning against overpriced AI-labeled stocks.

On the defense and government side, the Pentagon has launched something called Project Meridian, a 120-day sprint to map out what future warfare will look like — spanning AI, autonomous systems, robotics, directed energy, and even biotechnology, stretching from underground operations to the Moon. It’s being co-led by Elon Musk, Anduril founder Palmer Luckey, and former House Speaker Newt Gingrich, with recommendations due by the end of January 2027. Separately, Admiral Samuel Paparo, head of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, is visiting India this weekend to strengthen defense ties, with talks expected to cover regional security and emerging technologies.

Switching to biotech, there’s a lot of movement in drug development and funding. Novartis has struck a licensing deal with China’s Abogen Biosciences, picking up rights to an mRNA-encoded T-cell engager along with options on other RNA-based programs — a sign of continued big pharma interest in mRNA platforms beyond vaccines. Vera Therapeutics will present late-breaking data on its drug Trutakna for IgA nephropathy at Kidney Week in Denver later this month, while ADARx Pharmaceuticals closed out its IPO with underwriters exercising their full option, bringing total proceeds to roughly $513 million — a strong signal of investor appetite for siRNA-based therapeutics. And in India, Eli Lilly’s CEO commented that the country has strong biotech talent and expertise, but said funding infrastructure still lags behind, even as Lilly looks to expand its weight-loss drug lineup and explore local partnerships.

Finally, a couple of lighter technology notes: Ninja has released a combined microwave and air fryer using something it calls Fusion Crisp technology, and early impressions suggest it actually solves one of the biggest complaints people have about regular microwaves. And if you’re in the market for a soundbar, there’s a deep-dive review out today analyzing over 500 user reviews across five Blaupunkt models to help figure out which one actually fits your setup.

That’s the rundown — plenty of AI soul-searching today, some solid biotech funding news, and a Pentagon project that sounds like it belongs in a sci-fi script.
