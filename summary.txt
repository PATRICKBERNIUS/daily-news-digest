Good morning. Let's start with artificial intelligence, because there's a lot swirling today, much of it about trust and risk rather than pure innovation.

Elon Musk kicked things off with a notably candid admission, telling followers on X that he fears falling victim to what he's calling "super persuasive" AI, warning that many people are going to get "one-shotted" by these systems. It's a striking concession from someone usually positioned as an AI booster, and it dovetails with a broader theme running through today's coverage: AI agents are becoming powerful enough to act on our behalf, and that's raising uncomfortable questions. A widely covered story followed a Toronto man who let Meta's new AI agent, Muse, handle a Facebook Marketplace listing for his old keyboard as an experiment. The takeaway across multiple outlets covering this is the same: these agents are only as capable and trustworthy as the personal data we hand them, and users keep getting surprised when the tools go further than they expected, accessing information or making decisions users didn't realize they'd authorized. In India, this same anxiety has a sharper edge, with experts warning about "rogue" AI agents that have already breached public websites and accessed confidential data at major companies including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, prompting calls for stronger accountability safeguards. South Korea is seeing a parallel version of this fight play out in government, where CEOs of five major banks were grilled by lawmakers over a wave of AI-assisted cyberattacks and questions about whether internal controls are keeping up.

On a more personal note, a Brazilian bricklayer's story is making the rounds as a cautionary tale about AI's emotional reach — he sold his possessions and traveled over 2,000 kilometers to meet an online girlfriend, only to discover she was entirely AI-generated. And in a thoughtful opinion piece, a writer argues AI is quietly reshaping motherhood, noting that 81% of parents now use AI for parenting support, with real questions about what that's doing to parental judgment and connection, not just children's development.

On the business side of AI, the infrastructure build-out continues at pace. Banco Santander signed a new three-year framework with Nvidia to deepen its AI partnership, while CoreWeave's CEO has now sold nearly a billion dollars in shares since the company's IPO last year, mostly through prearranged trading plans, a detail likely to draw scrutiny given how hot AI infrastructure stocks have been. Speaking of infrastructure, Aeroflex Industries just secured a patent for automated 3D hose modeling technology, and notably said it's strategically expanding into liquid cooling systems for AI data centers, a sign of how much secondary industry is now orienting around AI's physical infrastructure needs. In India specifically, IIM Jammu signed a partnership with a US university to advance healthcare AI research, and the Indian Navy's Vice Chief of Naval Staff said the force is pushing development across air, sea, underwater, AI, and quantum domains to build a multi-domain network force, a push that's gained urgency amid ongoing regional tensions in West Asia.

Switching to biotech and health, there's meaningful news on the drug development front. GSK released its first global data on an experimental lung cancer drug, risvutatug rezetecan, showing positive anti-tumor activity in patients with advanced small cell lung cancer. Spyre Therapeutics presented new clinical and preclinical data at a major European gastroenterology conference in Barcelona, covering three antibody therapies being tested both alone and in combination for inflammatory bowel disease. And in a notable domestic policy move, the US Department of War — yes, that's now handling this — committed an $80 million conditional loan to biotech company Antheia to boost domestic production of pharmaceutical ingredients, part of a broader push to reduce reliance on foreign drug supply chains.

There's also a bigger, more philosophical biotech story worth flagging: scientists are raising alarms about "mirror life," a theoretical biotechnology involving mirror-image biological molecules, with researchers calling for global guardrails before anyone attempts to create it, citing serious biosecurity risks. That concern seems to be feeding into geopolitics too, since China has proposed new export controls on a dozen solar-manufacturing technologies, explicitly folding in restrictions touching AI and biotechnology as part of a broader national security push.

A few other items worth knowing about: the International Atomic Energy Agency warned that the world's aging nuclear reactor fleet could complicate global plans to expand nuclear capacity, since countries will need to extend old plants' lives while also building new ones simultaneously. Foreign banks are increasingly favoring India over China for expansion, as China's high capital requirements and tough competition push them elsewhere. And India's NDTV World Summit is set for October 22nd and 23rd in New Delhi, with Prime Minister Modi headlining a gathering of global policymakers and business leaders discussing the shifting world order.

That's the briefing — plenty of AI trust and infrastructure news today, alongside steady but significant movement in biotech drug development and some bigger-picture warnings about where both fields could be headed.
