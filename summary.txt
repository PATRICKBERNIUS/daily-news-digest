Good morning. Let's get you caught up on what's moving today, and there's a lot happening on the AI and chips front.

The biggest storyline is the fallout from Nvidia's decision to officially stop shipping its Hopper-series AI chips to China. Interim CEO Colette Kress confirmed the halt, and it's raising real questions about the future of the special licensing arrangement Washington and Beijing had negotiated to allow limited chip sales. This comes as Nvidia deals with broader corporate turbulence, and a Microsoft executive is now suggesting that drama around the company could actually push customers toward rival AI chips. That's notable because Microsoft, Amazon, and Google are all building their own AI chips partly to reduce their dependence on Nvidia in the first place. Meanwhile, the Trump administration is pressing ahead with restrictions on Chinese-made equipment inside US AI data centers, despite pushback from Amazon, Google, and Meta, who say ripping out those components could be a massive undertaking. Malaysia, caught in the middle of all this, is seeking clarity from the US on chip export rules and investigating possible re-exports of restricted hardware. So the global chip supply chain is clearly in a tense, unsettled moment right now.

On the corporate earnings side, Tencent's quarterly revenue growth slowed, but its cloud business got a lift from AI demand, which investors are watching closely as a signal of whether all that AI spending is actually paying off or just eating into margins.

There's also a broader cultural and political reckoning with AI happening. Cambridge Dictionary picked "human" as its word of the year for 2026, reaching back to the word's 15th-century roots to capture growing anxiety about identity, creativity, and what sets us apart in an AI-saturated world. That anxiety is showing up in policy circles too. Canada's Prime Minister Mark Carney warned that the lack of global governance on AI is an "enormous risk," citing economic, biohazard, democratic, and human dignity concerns. And the man who chairs the eurozone's finance ministers echoed that alarm, saying AI is outrunning governments' ability to regulate it, and that his biggest fear now is the effect this is having on government borrowing costs.

Switching to transportation tech, Waymo has pulled its robotaxis off freeways across all 15 of its US markets after California regulators imposed new safety restrictions, following repeated incidents involving construction zones and highway patrol vehicles. It's another setback for the company as it tries to prove out the reliability of autonomous driving at highway speeds.

Now to biotech and health research, where there's a lot of forward movement. Biocon founder Kiran Mazumdar-Shaw made the case that AI could transform drug discovery by speeding up diagnosis and cutting the time it takes to bring new treatments to patients, though she flagged that biotech startups still need patient, long-term capital to get there. In India, Prime Minister Modi launched an ambitious project at IIT Madras to build 3D cellular-resolution maps of more than 100 human brains, which would be the largest effort of its kind in the world and could significantly advance research into brain disease. Also in India, the Centre for Cellular and Molecular Platforms in Bengaluru signed a new cross-border collaboration with France's Genopole to deepen biotech and life sciences research ties, and ICMR held its third Health Research Excellence Summit in New Delhi, highlighting that it's now completed 100 successful technology transfer deals turning homegrown health research into real-world solutions.

On the clinical and drug development front, City of Hope and Adagene launched a Phase 1 trial testing a new combination therapy for advanced colorectal cancer in patients who haven't responded to standard treatments. Ichnos Glenmark Innovation filed for FDA approval to begin trials on a new multispecific immune therapy targeting solid tumors, engaging both T cells and natural killer cells. MAIA Biotechnology reported strong efficacy data from its expanded Phase 2 trial of a telomere-targeting lung cancer drug, which has FDA Fast Track status and could be headed toward an accelerated approval filing. And Nuclera, working with University of Cambridge researchers, demonstrated a faster method for analyzing hard-to-study drug targets using a cell-free protein technology approach, which could speed up early-stage drug development broadly.

A few other smaller items worth a mention: Tufts University opened a new innovation hub in Medford for food and materials startups, aiming to grow the biotech sector in Massachusetts with projects ranging from insect-based pet food to lab-grown fabric. And Thailand's science and technology agency flagged AI, biotech, advanced materials, and clean energy as the emerging technologies it thinks the country needs to lean into to tackle climate change, pollution, and an aging population.

That's the rundown — chips and AI governance dominating the macro picture, with biotech and health research quietly racking up some real clinical progress underneath it all.
