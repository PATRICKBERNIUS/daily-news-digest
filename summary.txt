Good morning. Let's start with the AI world, because there's a lot moving there today, and some of it is genuinely unsettling for markets. Wall Street had a rough Friday, with the Nasdaq posting its biggest single-day drop since June after Intel and Amazon earnings raised fresh doubts about whether the massive spending on AI infrastructure is actually paying off. That selloff comes right as Anthropic's IPO filing is making the rounds, and analysts are picking apart what's missing from the paperwork as much as what's in it, since there are real open questions about the health of the business and the wider AI sector's economics. Adding to the "is this bubble getting weird" vibe, Sam Altman stirred controversy by suggesting that people shouldn't own personal computer memory at all, arguing that RAM belongs in data centers where it can be fully utilized rather than sitting idle in someone's laptop.

On the more futuristic side, Google says its Project Suncatcher has hit a milestone: they got an AI model running on a prototype satellite in orbit and received back its first complete response, including a haiku about the cosmos generated using the satellite's onboard chip. It's an early step toward the idea of running AI computing infrastructure in space, with thermal and radiation stress tests still to come.

Governments and institutions are also scrambling to keep pace with AI's spread. Canada and the European Union launched a new strategic alliance out of Montreal covering critical minerals, energy, AI, digital trade and defense cooperation, a sign of how central AI has become to geopolitical and economic strategy. Relatedly, a NATO commander is pushing Canada specifically to invest more in AI and military drones as the country ramps up defense spending. In India, the Data Security Council has released a liability guide specifically for agentic AI, aimed at sorting out who's responsible when an AI agent goes rogue or acts outside its intended duties. And in California, there's a growing industry of AI "evaluators," professionals hired to audit AI systems on behalf of the very companies that built them, raising obvious questions about independence and whether this kind of self-policing can actually catch serious risks before they cause harm. Meanwhile in Malaysia, a entrepreneurs' association is making the opposite case, arguing that AI support shouldn't just be for big players and that small businesses like restaurants and retailers need accessible AI tools to manage rising costs and stay competitive.

On the security front, the Binance hack is being called the largest cyber heist in history, at six point three billion dollars, and it's a warning sign for where crypto crime is heading, since investigators say attackers are increasingly using AI and other advanced techniques to break in and launder stolen funds almost instantly. Separately, there's a wave of art thefts hitting smaller museums across Europe, with a recent nine million euro heist at Italy's Fondazione Magnani Rocca highlighting how many cultural institutions still lack basic security and emergency planning, pushing authorities to invest more in surveillance technology.

Now to biotech and health, where there's quite a bit of corporate movement. Aspen Neuroscience priced its IPO, raising around one hundred forty million dollars to fund its autologous cell therapy work for Parkinson's disease. On the flip side, Annovis Bio, which is working on a Phase 3 treatment for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, got a notice from the New York Stock Exchange that it's fallen out of compliance with listing standards due to its market cap and stockholders' equity dropping below fifty million dollars. And in the skin disease space, Pelthos Therapeutics is combining with Castle Creek Biosciences to form a new company focused on serious skin diseases, anchored by a late-stage therapy targeting a rare condition called dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa.

On the research side, there's a genuinely strange and fascinating finding out of Aarhus University: scientists discovered that bacteria can keep producing DNA even after they're dead, powered by dormant viruses called bacteriophages sitting inside the dead cells. Researchers used synthetic DNA to prove the dead bacteria could still synthesize new genetic material, which could open doors in environmental biotechnology and bioenergy production down the line.

A few smaller technology notes worth knowing. Delhi's municipal corporation is racing to get a new automated, RFID-based toll system running at twenty major entry points before an October 31st deadline, aimed at cutting congestion and pollution at the city's borders. In the AI-and-safety-on-the-ground category, researchers are finding that while AI is increasingly used to flag hazards on construction sites, there's still limited hard evidence that it's actually reducing injuries. And in India, Panasonic has partnered with Anacity to bring smart-living technology to residential communities and gated complexes.

Finally, a quick note on markets, Morgan Stanley maintained a buy rating on Luxshare Precision with a price target well above its current trading level, and analysts also turned bullish on Telesat and Twilio, so some pockets of tech investing remain upbeat even as the broader AI-driven market mood took a hit this week.

That's the rundown for today.
