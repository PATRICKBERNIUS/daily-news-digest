Good morning. Let's start with the AI world, where today's theme is really about money, jobs, and who's paying for all this investment. Apple is reportedly tightening the purse strings on AI tool usage for its own employees, slashing daily spending limits from $300 down to $200, with talk of going as low as $100, as the company tries to control ballooning AI costs. Meanwhile, in Australia, Canva just announced it's cutting more than a fifth of its workforce as part of a major restructuring driven by AI's disruption of its business model — over 1,270 jobs affected. On the flip side, Maersk is actually raising its profit guidance for the third time this year, crediting AI investment along with high freight rates and strong demand for keeping its shipping business humming. And in the markets, European tech stocks lagged today on growing concern about U.S. regulatory scrutiny of AI infrastructure spending, even as mining stocks and ArcelorMittal's strong earnings lifted the broader European market. That nervousness about an AI bubble is also showing up in India, where veteran banker Uday Kotak suggested that if the global AI investment boom cools off, it could actually work in India's favor by redirecting foreign capital into Indian markets. Separately, a new report finds AI could add $79 billion to India's GDP over the next decade, with young Indians showing strong enthusiasm for adopting the technology.

On the regulatory and safety side, Google announced it's switching off its AI tools for schoolchildren by default, walking back its push to get AI features into classrooms after facing criticism that it was moving faster than schools and educators were ready for. And in a stranger AI-adjacent story, a former FBI agent accused of stealing about a million dollars in cryptocurrency from an adversarial nation is set to plead guilty Thursday — he reportedly used an AI chatbot to help plan his escape to Portugal. There's also a thoughtful piece out today urging people to be cautious about using AI for medical and dental advice, a reminder that convenience shouldn't replace professional judgment when it comes to health decisions.

In geopolitics and business, China has blocked SoftBank's $5.4 billion deal to acquire ABB's robotics division, a setback for Masayoshi Son's ambitions to make SoftBank a central player in AI and robotics.

Turning to technology and research more broadly, there's a neat story out of the University of Nottingham, where researchers have built a self-driving robotic lab that uses AI to automate the discovery of heat-resistant coatings for aerospace and energy applications — essentially letting robots run thousands of material experiments to speed up innovation. Michelin, meanwhile, has launched a joint research lab with several French universities and the CNRS, investing about €4.5 million over four years into new tire technology and advanced composite materials. And on the corporate tech services side, Hexaware has landed a five-year strategic technology partnership with the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, focused on AI integration across the bank's systems, while Unilever is pouring an additional €35 million into its R&D hubs in Mumbai and Bengaluru to boost AI-driven innovation and biotechnology work, building toward turning its Mumbai center into a next-generation product innovation hub.

In biotech and health research, there's encouraging early data from Ethris, a Munich-based biotech, whose nasally administered mRNA therapy for asthma patients showed a 40-fold reduction in rhinovirus viral load compared to placebo, with signs it could reduce asthma flare-ups triggered by colds — a promising step for a currently hard-to-treat trigger of exacerbations. In neuroscience, researchers in South Korea, led by teams at DGIST and partner institutions, have developed a new deep-learning system called NONOS that can precisely separate brain rhythm signals from background noise, which could meaningfully improve adaptive deep brain stimulation therapies for conditions like Parkinson's.

On the corporate and financial side of biotech, it's a busy day for clinical-stage companies. Annovis Bio, which is developing a Phase 3 treatment for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, announced a new public stock offering to raise capital. Jasper Therapeutics is executing a 1-for-30 reverse stock split effective Friday. And several companies — including Climb Bio and Ichnos Glenmark Innovation — are gearing up to present new clinical data at the upcoming American Society of Hematology meeting in December, covering treatments for immune thrombocytopenia and a novel trispecific antibody therapy for relapsed multiple myeloma. Unicycive Therapeutics will also be presenting at a healthcare investment conference next week.

Finally, in India, regulators at SEBI have formed an 18-member working group to overhaul listing rules for startups in deep-tech, biotech, semiconductors, and space — an effort aimed at making it easier for innovation-driven companies to raise capital on public markets. And rounding out the economic picture, India's warehousing sector posted strong 12% growth last quarter, driven by manufacturing and e-commerce demand, a sign that the broader industrial and logistics economy continues to expand even as the AI investment story dominates headlines elsewhere.
