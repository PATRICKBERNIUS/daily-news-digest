Good morning. Let's get you up to speed on a busy news cycle, with AI dominating headlines once again, alongside some notable biotech progress and a major tech infrastructure stumble.

Starting with the big picture on artificial intelligence: the financial world is growing increasingly nervous about the AI boom. A Federal Reserve survey found that 87 percent of experts now see AI as the top threat to financial stability, citing inflated equity valuations, the risk of a sharp market correction, and cybersecurity vulnerabilities tied to AI systems. That anxiety is compounded by numbers showing just how much debt Big Tech is taking on to fund the AI race. Global AI-linked debt has hit roughly 687 billion dollars, with five major hyperscalers including Alphabet and Amazon issuing nearly 300 billion dollars in debt this year alone, on top of 1.7 trillion dollars in lease obligations. Investors are reportedly getting pickier about how exposed they want to be to AI spending going forward. On the raw materials side, there's a reminder that AI's growth isn't just digital — it depends heavily on metals like galvanized steel and other inputs needed to build out data centers, raising questions about whether metal markets are the next bottleneck or the next big winner in this buildout.

On the policy and governance front, the Philippines just signed an executive order adopting a national AI framework, joining a growing list of countries trying to get ahead of regulation. Meanwhile, a much heavier topic is being debated in Geneva this week, where the United Nations is holding a conference on autonomous weapons — AI-powered systems capable of making lethal decisions without human input. There's currently no real legal framework governing these so-called killer robots, and experts are warning that diplomatic talks are moving far slower than the technology itself, raising serious ethical and security concerns.

On the consumer side, Samsung has officially launched its new AI smart glasses, developed with Google and designed with Gentle Monster and Warby Parker, bringing Gemini AI into everyday eyewear. And in India, healthcare innovator Dr. Devi Shetty is predicting that AI, robotic surgery, and efficiency gains could cut healthcare costs by as much as 50 percent over the next five to seven years, potentially making India the first country to fully separate quality care from wealth.

Now to the tech outage story that caused real-world chaos this week. The U.S. experienced its longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years, canceling thousands of flights and stranding passengers, including sports teams scrambling for transportation. Officials say new air traffic control technology overloaded aging systems, causing communication and radar gaps. This came right on the heels of a separate major Microsoft outage that knocked out both consumer and enterprise services for hours before recovering. Together, these two incidents have sparked a broader conversation about just how fragile our infrastructure has become given how dependent everything — from air travel to email — now is on a handful of interconnected tech systems.

Switching to biotech, there's encouraging clinical news out of the neurofibromatosis space. Pasithea Therapeutics presented strong Phase 1 data for its drug PAS-004, showing major reductions in tumor size for patients with cutaneous neurofibromas, with some patients seeing nearly 50 to 70 percent reduction over time, and promising early signals in the harder-to-treat plexiform form of the disease. The FDA has already cleared the drug to move into Phase 2 trials. On the business side, Windward Bio had a strong Nasdaq debut, with shares jumping 68 percent on its first trading day, valuing the Basel-based antibody drug developer at nearly 1.7 billion dollars — a sign that investor appetite for biotech IPOs remains healthy. There's also continued movement in gene therapy, with Andelyn Biosciences partnering with South Korea's Celliaz to advance a new AAV-based treatment for retinitis pigmentosa, and in France, HTL Biotechnology opened a new 15-million-euro production facility dedicated to pharmaceutical-grade DNA components, aimed at meeting rising global demand in regenerative medicine.

Finally, a few interesting innovation stories worth a mention. In Arizona, engineers found that a buried mesh technology called geogrid kept a forest road intact and pothole-free for five years, while untreated sections deteriorated badly — a small but telling example of infrastructure innovation paying off. In China, a company called Yanhe Technology launched experimental perovskite solar panels into orbit to test how they perform in space, a step that could matter for the future of satellite and spacecraft power. And in India, Tamil Nadu is rolling out dedicated commercial drone corridors across ten districts for deliveries and agricultural use, while Mysuru just opened an advanced 8K LED planetarium, inaugurated by the Prime Minister, aimed at boosting science education and astronomy tourism in the region.

That's the rundown — a mix of big AI anxiety, infrastructure fragility, and some genuinely hopeful biotech progress today.
