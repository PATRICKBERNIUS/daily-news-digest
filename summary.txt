Good morning. Let's start with what's happening in AI, because there's a lot of movement on the infrastructure and money side of things today. The big theme is power — tech companies are so desperate for electricity to run their data centers that Google is now reportedly offering to pay other companies to help secure power supply, joining a broader scramble that's included restarting old nuclear plants and cobbling together off-grid gas generators. At the same time, the communities hosting these massive data centers are pushing back, and in response, tech giants are promising to be more transparent, take fewer tax breaks, and funnel more money into the towns that host their server farms. Meanwhile, Nvidia's Jensen Huang, speaking at a conference in Berlin, said demand for so-called neoclouds — companies that provide dedicated AI computing capacity — is far exceeding expectations, but financing constraints are limiting how fast they can actually grow. That tension between booming demand and shaky financing seems to be rattling markets too — tech stocks sold off in New York overnight on AI sector worries, which rippled into Asian markets, even though London's FTSE is expected to edge higher today.

On the AI application side, there's a steady drumbeat of companies building AI into everyday business functions. In India, Honasa Consumer has named a new Chief Product and Technology Officer specifically to push "agentic AI" into its operations, and IndusInd Securities has a new CEO who says data and AI will be central to modernizing the firm. NEC has developed a vision-language AI model aimed at detecting early signs of natural disasters from visual feeds in real time, which is a genuinely interesting use case for public safety. On the edge-computing front, a company called Artery is pushing AI capabilities out of the cloud and into smaller devices like sensors and voice assistants. There's also a cultural angle worth mentioning: France has launched a literary prize specifically for AI-assisted fiction, which signals growing mainstream acceptance of AI as a creative collaborator rather than just a tool.

On regulation and public trust, London's Oxford Street is about to roll out live facial recognition cameras to scan holiday shoppers starting in December, and human rights groups are already criticizing it as unregulated surveillance. Separately, European businesses are voicing frustration with American tech companies, questioning how much real control they have over the technology they depend on — a sign that transatlantic tension over tech dependency is far from resolved. And in India, there's continued momentum in building out AI ecosystems regionally — Goa is positioning itself as the next AI hub after Bengaluru, aiming to train 50,000 AI-ready workers by 2030, while Karnataka's government is deepening its investment in ARTPARK, including a new robotics and AI innovation campus called BRAINZ in Bengaluru.

Now to biotech and health, where there's some genuinely significant clinical news. Vera Therapeutics presented final results from its ORIGIN 3 trial for a drug called TRUTAKNA, treating IgA nephropathy, a kidney disease — the drug hit statistical significance on all its key endpoints and the data was published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine, which is about as strong a validation as you get. The company plans to seek full FDA approval in 2027. On the corporate side, a planned $34 billion merger between Johnson & Johnson's orthopaedics unit and Smith & Nephew has collapsed, reportedly over a dispute about who would lead the combined company — a reminder that even strategically sound deals can fall apart over leadership egos. Elsewhere, Ping An Biomedical is acquiring a trio of oncology-related patents to build out its intellectual property portfolio, Replimune will report quarterly earnings next week, and Zylidac Bio has partnered with Prolific Machines to scale up a next-generation biologics manufacturing platform.

There's also a broader investment story: Moderna and SanDisk are apparently leading a pack of ten U.S. stocks that have more than tripled in value this year, with biotech and tech dominating the list of 2026's biggest market winners. And on the workforce side, India's healthcare sector is expected to create 67,000 new jobs by 2026 through its Global Capability Centers, with most of that demand aimed at experienced, mid-career professionals, while IIT Madras has launched a new "finishing school" program called UDYOG to give biotechnology graduates hands-on manufacturing training before they enter the workforce.

Finally, a bit of politics crossing over into biotech — Donald Trump has publicly defended Vivek Ramaswamy, the biotech entrepreneur turned Ohio gubernatorial candidate, after backlash over a controversial attack ad targeting his opponent Amy Acton's family. That race is shaping up to be a notable one to watch given Ramaswamy's business background.

That's the rundown — plenty of movement on AI power and money questions, incremental but meaningful biotech wins, and a lot of institutional repositioning around AI skills and infrastructure globally.
