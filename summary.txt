Good morning. Let's start with the biggest story of the day, which is the growing anxiety around AI's financial footing on Wall Street. Reuters and other outlets are reporting that investors are scrambling to understand just how much AI exposure is hiding in their portfolios after a recent selloff rattled confidence. This ties directly into a broader story about an "IPO bloodbath" — the ten largest US stock market debuts are now down an average of 20.5% from their offering prices, with SpaceX losing momentum and Anthropic's potential $64 billion IPO being watched as the real test of whether Wall Street still has appetite for AI bets. Adding fuel to this, Moody's chief economist Mark Zandi says AI is genuinely driving economic growth and job creation, but he's also warning it's contributing to inflation by pushing up borrowing costs and prices tied to the AI buildout. So the overall picture is one of a market that's still bullish on AI long-term but increasingly nervous about how overheated valuations have gotten.

On the regulatory and safety side, there's a notable opinion piece pushing for a worldwide AI treaty, arguing that while bipartisan efforts in Congress to regulate AI are encouraging, data centers and AI's environmental and public health costs mean no single country can govern this alone. That concern feels less abstract given a striking story out of Ghana, where researchers at a small AI lab discovered just how easily safety guardrails can fail — they stripped the restrictions off a drug discovery model and it readily generated designs for over a thousand proteins and thousands of small molecules that could function as toxins. It's a sobering reminder of how dual-use AI tools can be, and why global oversight is being pushed so hard right now.

On a lighter note, AI's reach keeps expanding into unexpected corners — perfumers are now using AI to speed up scent development and experiment with unusual ingredient combinations, raising the inevitable question of whether an algorithm can ever truly replace a human nose.

In biotech and pharma, there's a lot of corporate movement. TRIMTECH Therapeutics has struck a research collaboration with Biogen to develop small-molecule degrader therapies aimed at neurodegenerative diseases, a space getting a lot of attention lately. 858 Therapeutics, an oncology-focused biotech, announced a leadership change, with cofounder Jeffrey Stafford stepping back from the CEO role to become executive chairman, handing the reins to Dr. Srini Ramanathan. Meanwhile STAT and WireScreen launched a new "China's Biotech Powerhouse 25" index to track rising Chinese pharma and life sciences players, notably excluding the big contract manufacturers like WuXi AppTec in favor of companies doing original drug discovery. And on the compliance side, a Pleasanton biotech company, Cyion Technologies, agreed to pay over $500,000 to settle Justice Department allegations that it wrongly received small business grants it wasn't eligible for.

In health tech, India's Sophrosyne Technologies completed post-silicon validation of what it says is India's first indigenous biomedical sensor chipset built for wearable health monitoring — a milestone for the country's push into homegrown semiconductor design at the intersection of healthcare and AI. Also in India, Manipal Hospitals in Bengaluru introduced Karnataka's first Toumai robotic surgery system, expanding the use of robotic-assisted surgery in the region. And ENCLONY announced an expansion into highly potent pharmaceutical manufacturing, supplying containment and inspection technology for tablet and capsule production to a European pharma manufacturer, FAREVA EXCELLA.

Rounding out the broader tech and infrastructure world, Malaysia is positioning itself for a bigger role in next-generation connectivity after winning re-election to the ITU Council, which it says opens the door to early discussions on 6G standards; separately, Malaysia is also pushing to become a leader in green energy through homegrown expertise rather than just adopting foreign technology. In Malaysia-adjacent business news, Citaglobal, TNB Genco, and TIZA AI have signed an agreement to explore building a massive off-grid power facility — up to 1,300 megawatts — to support a planned AI data center park in Pahang, a sign of just how much energy infrastructure is now being built specifically around AI demand. There's also a legal spat brewing between Jaguar Land Rover and China's BYD, with JLR vowing to protect its intellectual property amid allegations that BYD copied the Defender's design. And in India, shipbuilding ambitions are growing too, with South Korean shipbuilders reportedly in talks for multi-billion-dollar projects to help India reduce its reliance on foreign-built vessels.

That's the roundup — plenty of movement in AI investment jitters, biotech dealmaking, and infrastructure being built around the AI boom, with a few reminders scattered throughout that the technology's risks are being taken just as seriously as its promise.
