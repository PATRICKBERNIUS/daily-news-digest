Good morning. Let's get you caught up on what's happening, and the biggest story today is really about whether the AI boom still has legs.

Starting in China, there's growing evidence that the AI investment bubble there has burst. The STAR50 index, which tracks Shanghai's homegrown tech and AI stocks, has wiped out roughly $480 billion in value over just four months, a drop of about 40 percent. Multiple reports are now confirming this pullback is hitting everything from private AI startups to state-backed computing projects across Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen. It's a sharp reversal for what had been the hottest trade on China's stock market this year, and it's raising uncomfortable questions about whether AI valuations got ahead of themselves globally, not just in China.

That tension between hype and reality is showing up everywhere else too. On one hand, money is still pouring into AI infrastructure at a staggering pace. Samsung's chairman is touring chip packaging labs in Japan to prepare for surging AI chip demand, and OpenAI's Sam Altman personally met with about 50 Samsung executives this weekend to shore up memory chip supply as the global chip crunch deepens. In Korea, KB Kookmin Bank just landed the lead role in financing a $9.45 billion AI computing infrastructure project led by Brookfield. Saudi Aramco closed a deal buying a 49 percent stake in the PIF-owned AI company Humain. And the Quad countries, the US, India, Japan, and Australia, pledged $85 million toward a "trusted AI" initiative to expand regional AI access. So capital is still flowing hard into the infrastructure layer of AI even as public valuations wobble.

On the other hand, you're seeing some real skepticism about whether all this AI spending is justified. There's an analysis questioning whether Bloom Energy, trading at 140 times earnings thanks to the AI-driven fuel cell boom, can actually grow fast enough to justify that price. And notably, Apple is positioning itself as the voice of restraint here. CEO John Ternus said plainly that Apple doesn't want to "ship AI for AI's sake," arguing the company would rather build things people genuinely want than chase the trend just to have a headline AI feature. It's a pointed contrast to the rest of the industry's spending spree.

Beyond the money story, there's a lot happening on how AI is actually being used. In healthcare, AI is now showing up in doctor's visits constantly, from ambient listening tools that write clinical notes to systems assisting with diagnosis, but experts are cautioning patients and doctors not to become overly reliant on it. Scotland's AI minister laid out a regulatory approach aligning with the EU while investing in chip design, trying to make sure AI benefits ordinary communities rather than just tech companies. And on the darker side, investigative journalist Mariana van Zeller went undercover into the world of romance scams for a new documentary, warning that AI is going to make these emotional manipulation schemes even harder to detect. In industrial applications, HD Hyundai Robotics is partnering with Palantir to use AI to cut robot programming costs by more than half, a good example of AI delivering concrete efficiency gains rather than hype.

Switching to biotech, there's a notable shift happening in China, where the biotech sector is moving beyond just manufacturing generic drugs and ingredients toward developing and licensing genuinely innovative pharmaceuticals internationally, signaling China wants a bigger seat at the global drug development table. Closer to home, a Baton Rouge biotech company called Phenomune has rolled out free at-home "innate immunity" test kits to 250,000 Louisiana residents, backed by $8 million in state funding, based on a local doctor's research into natural immune response. There's also an interesting trend piece on "biotech beauty," where skincare companies are moving away from plant-based ingredients toward lab-engineered, science-backed compounds as the next frontier past niacinamide and retinol.

On the corporate biotech side, a couple of smaller names are in the news for less exciting reasons: Annovis Bio, which is working on an Alzheimer's and Parkinson's drug, got a notice from the NYSE that it's fallen out of compliance with listing standards due to its market cap and stockholders' equity dropping below $50 million. And Dyadic International announced it'll report third-quarter earnings and host an investor call on November 12th.

Finally, a few smaller items worth knowing: IIM Bangalore reported 100 percent summer placements for its latest class, with Google and Microsoft among the recruiters and technology hiring doubling year over year, a solid signal of continued tech sector demand for young talent. And IIT Delhi's annual Open House drew 4,500 visitors and showcased more than 500 research projects, reflecting strong momentum in India's academic research scene.

That's the rundown, overall the throughline today is a widening gap between AI's long-term infrastructure buildout, which is still accelerating, and a growing wave of doubt about near-term valuations, especially out of China.
