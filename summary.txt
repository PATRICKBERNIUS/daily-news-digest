Good morning. Let's start with artificial intelligence, because there's a lot of tension today between AI's economic promise and growing concerns about its risks and valuation.

On the financial front, the IMF is warning that the AI boom could be setting markets up for a sharp correction. Their concern is that expectations have run well ahead of actual productivity gains, and if that gap becomes obvious, losses could spread globally, not just in the US. That warning is already playing out in the markets — Goldman Sachs data shows global hedge funds have taken steep losses as investors unwind AI-related trades, with Asia-focused funds hit hardest, down nearly seven percent in early October. Against that backdrop, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh struck a more measured tone, declining to signal the Fed's next move on interest rates but calling AI a "positive supply shock" for the economy over the medium term. His comments seem to have calmed nerves somewhat, with European stock futures pointing higher Friday as the recent tech selloff eased.

On the regulatory and safety side, OpenAI is facing a serious legal challenge, with at least eight US states suing the company, alleging its chatbot has harmed children and deceived adults through reckless design choices. Separately, there's a growing conversation about AI in the workplace, with experts warning that worker protections need to catch up to the spread of AI-driven surveillance tools — Amazon's tracking system came up specifically as an example drawing scrutiny. And in South Korea, AI-powered smart glasses are creating a headache for teachers ahead of the national college entrance exam, as schools scramble to detect and prevent a new form of high-tech cheating.

On the product and research side, Meta released a new open-weight AI model called Muse Spark Open, built for coding and complex reasoning, notable because it's powerful enough to run on just two Apple Mac Studio desktops rather than a massive data center. Nvidia is opening an AI hub in Stockholm aimed at startups working on health and climate problems, pairing its hardware with a biotech demonstration lab. And in a genuinely big computing story, Google says it's cracked a twenty-year-old engineering bottleneck in quantum computing. Their Willow processor used a technique called "magic state cultivation" to cut error rates roughly 40-fold, a key step toward making quantum computers useful for real-world, universal calculations rather than just narrow demonstrations.

Now to biotech and health research, where there's a steady drumbeat of deals and discoveries. City Therapeutics, an RNA interference drug developer, priced an upsized IPO at $200 million, a sign that investor appetite for clinical-stage biotech hasn't dried up. Taiwan's Jyong Biotech is planning a new manufacturing facility in Texas to support future US drug commercialization. In cancer treatment, Adlai Nortye struck trial collaboration deals with both Eli Lilly and Merck for its experimental tumor drug AN4035, and separately, IBA and Framatome are forming a joint venture to industrialize production of Astatine-211, a rare radioactive isotope used in next-generation cancer therapies. On the agricultural biotech side, Bayer is partnering with Absolute's INERA unit to expand biological crop nutrition products in India, and Herbalife India launched a plant biotechnology accelerator with IIT Madras to push research in that space toward real products. There's also a neat basic-science discovery out of the University of Miami, where researchers identified a previously unknown group of viruses that appear to bridge an evolutionary gap between viruses and cellular protein structures, offering new clues about the origins of life and potential biotech applications. And in New Zealand, Daisy Lab, a startup making animal-free dairy protein in the lab, is planning international expansion after being acquired by a UK company.

Beyond AI and biotech, a few other things worth knowing. Tesla unveiled a redesigned Roadster with SpaceX-style thrusters that can supposedly flip the car mid-air, but production has slipped again, now targeted for 2028, and this comes just before an earnings report investors care about more. Apple's first foldable phone, the iPhone Duo, opens for pre-order in India today at a steep price point of nearly three lakh rupees. And a California company called Reflect Orbital has a wild plan to sell on-demand sunlight at night by placing giant mirrors in orbit, using launch services from India's Skyroot Aerospace. Finally, on a more disruptive note, Air India and Air India Express have suspended flights to several Saudi Arabian cities until October 20 due to evolving security concerns in the region, so worth checking travel plans if that affects you.

That's the briefing for today.
