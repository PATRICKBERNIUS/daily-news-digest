Good morning. Let's get you caught up, and today it's almost entirely about one thing: the sheer scale of the AI boom and the growing anxiety around it.

Start with Wall Street, because the numbers are staggering. S&P 500 companies are on track for profit growth north of 30 percent this quarter, and a huge chunk of that is coming from a handful of tech giants—Alphabet, Amazon, Meta—pouring money into AI infrastructure. Reuters is framing this as a genuine historic moment, saying the AI buildout is set to eclipse even the dot-com era telecom investment boom. It's creating what they're calling a "wealth boom and price shock" collision—massive gains for investors and tech companies, but real cost pressures rippling through the broader economy. Nvidia remains the poster child for this, with pieces still circulating on how early investors have turned modest stakes into life-changing returns, and people are now calculating just how many shares you'd need to become a millionaire off the stock.

But there are cracks showing in how sustainable this all is. In Asia, the collapse of Firmus Grid's IPO is being read as a warning shot—investors are suddenly a lot less willing to bankroll AI infrastructure deals that are purely bets on future earnings rather than current performance. That's likely to make the whole pipeline of Asian AI listings tougher to pull off going forward. Meanwhile, Amazon is reportedly circling a $7 billion acquisition of AI startup Decart—notably, a deal Anthropic walked away from after offering a billion less—which tells you something about how aggressively the big players are still shopping, even as skepticism grows elsewhere.

And that skepticism extends to safety and regulation. Microsoft's president made a pointed comment to the BBC that's getting a lot of attention: he says milk currently faces more regulatory scrutiny than artificial intelligence does, and he's calling for urgent global oversight with independent evaluators to vet new AI systems before they're deployed. This comes as companies like Anthropic and OpenAI have started voluntarily disclosing when their AI agents behave in unexpected or "rogue" ways, part of a broader and increasingly serious conversation about long-term risks. On a more optimistic note, TikTok's CEO Shou Zi Chew is predicting the flip side of that coin—he thinks AI will deliver real scientific breakthroughs within two to three years, pointing to ByteDance's own research into AI models for predicting biomolecular structures as an early sign of what's coming in medicine and science.

On the infrastructure side, the AI boom is also reshaping energy and even space investment. Trinasolar just launched new solar and battery storage tech in Vietnam explicitly aimed at powering AI data centers, which are becoming massive electricity consumers. And investment firm Finaltis is now pointing to space as potentially the next big theme after AI, arguing that falling launch costs are opening commercial space up the same way cloud computing once did.

Closer to the ground, AI is showing up in more everyday and institutional ways. L&T Technology Services opened an AI-native lab in Bengaluru focused on digital manufacturing, using Nvidia-powered digital twins and autonomous systems. IIT Kharagpur is rolling out online AI certificate courses for graduates and professionals. India's Railway Protection Force is spending roughly 125 crore rupees on new tech to modernize crime prevention. And on the consumer side, a company called Pebble launched a subscription-free wellness band that uses AI to track things like "body age" and recovery readiness.

Outside the AI storm, a couple of other notable items: the Nobel Prize in Economics went to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford for their work on macroeconomic models explaining stability through booms and recessions—fitting, given everything else happening in markets right now. And on a more human note, author Amish Tripathi and a panel of educators made a public push for parents to prioritize reading with kids at home, warning that rising screen time shouldn't come at the expense of imagination and real human interaction.

That's the rundown—AI money, AI anxiety, and AI everywhere in between.
