Good morning. Let's start with the AI world, where there's a lot happening on the money, infrastructure, and regulatory fronts. Morgan Stanley and Japan's MUFG are preparing to sell off a massive $4.5 billion loan tied to CoreWeave, the AI infrastructure company that's been building out computing power for Meta. It's another sign of just how much capital is flowing into the data center buildout behind the AI boom. But that boom is creating real bottlenecks — companies around the world are now scrambling for memory chips, with the Wall Street Journal reporting procurement teams setting up literal war rooms, flying to Seoul, and even trawling eBay to secure supply, because AI data centers are soaking up so much of the global chip market. On the regulatory side, Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey is calling for an international effort to test AI models more rigorously, both before and after they're released, echoing growing concern among policymakers about keeping pace with the technology's risks.

Speaking of risks, there's rising public unease about AI's role in pricing. Digital price tags are rapidly replacing paper ones in grocery stores, and AI tools are increasingly being used to set prices based on predicted customer behavior. Terms like "dynamic pricing" and "surveillance pricing" are now stirring real alarm among consumers worried about being quietly charged more based on what an algorithm thinks they'll pay.

On the more hopeful side of AI applications, a new report suggests India is uniquely positioned to lead the development of affordable, scalable healthcare AI for the Global South, potentially transforming care delivery in resource-limited settings. There's also an interesting piece out of South Africa exploring how AI might make divorce proceedings less adversarial, given how the legal process is typically built around conflict rather than compromise. And in the enterprise space, a new platform called Concierto AI has launched globally, offering what it calls "sovereign AI" — letting companies run frontier-grade AI tools internally within their own cloud or data center rather than relying on outside providers.

On the corporate and labor side of tech, IBM has decided to halt H-1B visa and green card sponsorship for most of its consulting positions, which could leave thousands of Indian tech professionals in the U.S. facing relocation or job loss. Separately, a Lenovo report finds that so-called "rugged" frontline devices often aren't living up to expectations, with outages, battery failures, and screen issues costing workers nearly two weeks of productivity a year.

In biotech and health research, India is making a lot of noise about its ambitions. Science Minister Jitendra Singh says the country's bio-economy is on track to hit $300 billion by 2030, with biotech startups growing from just 50 in 2014 to more than 11,000 today. He's also pushing industry to invest in India's genetic diversity as a resource for global research and new business opportunities in genomics and healthcare.

On the medical technology front, Elon Musk's Neuralink has gotten FDA approval for a major human trial of its brain-computer interface chip, pushing the idea of controlling computers with your thoughts a step closer to reality. In reproductive medicine, Taiwan's NUWA Healthcare hosted a major forum bringing together fertility specialists from dozens of clinics to discuss advances in embryo testing and IVF techniques. And on the pharmaceutical supply side, biotech company Pharming has paused manufacturing of its rare-disease drug RUCONEST after a quality-control issue at its manufacturing partner, warning of likely supply interruptions in the U.S., though they say patients currently on the product aren't affected yet.

Finally, a couple of broader notes: U.S. stock markets remain under pressure, with the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both in correction territory, and there's continued unease tied to policy signals out of Washington. And on a lighter note, a Dublin-based company, InnaLabs, played a key role in guiding a European Space Agency spacecraft nearly 200 million kilometers away, helping prove out technology that could one day deflect a planet-killing asteroid — a nice reminder that not all the big tech news this year is about chips and algorithms.

That's the briefing for today.
