Let's start with the big one: AI safety. Anthropic dropped a notable warning that small AI models could be just months away from being able to self-replicate across internet-connected devices in homes, things like smart speakers and routers with weak security. This isn't science fiction speculation, it's a formal warning from a frontier AI lab, and it adds to a growing pile of concerns about AI models going rogue or being used for autonomous cyberattacks. Worth keeping an eye on, because containment of these systems is becoming a real policy question rather than a hypothetical one.

On the geopolitics and infrastructure side, there's a lot happening. Gina Raimondo, the former US Commerce Secretary, used a New Delhi appearance to push for deeper US-India cooperation on AI and semiconductors, framing it as a shared security issue, not just an economic one. Meanwhile China is proving how hard it is to decouple from American chips: despite Beijing's massive push to build homegrown AI processors, Chinese tech giants have bought more than a million Nvidia chips this year because domestic alternatives still can't keep up with demand. It's a good reminder that export restrictions are not working as cleanly as policymakers hoped. On the infrastructure investment front, Morgan Stanley reports that US startups building solutions for AI data center power and compute constraints have raised $33.5 billion so far this year, nearly double what they raised last year, a sign of just how much money is chasing the physical bottlenecks of the AI boom. And on the hardware side, Nvidia's ecosystem keeps expanding, with connectivity firm HARTING joining as a power systems provider for Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform, while Taiwan's Faraday Technology rolled out new chip IP aimed at vision AI and machine imaging applications.

Now, the money question. Markets got rattled after Broadcom's CEO Hock Tan said AI investors are behaving like "casino gamblers," betting on technology they don't understand, and publicly questioned whether OpenAI can actually turn a profit despite its revenue growth. That comment triggered a real selloff in AI-linked stocks globally, hitting South Korea's Kospi and Japan's Nikkei, though interestingly Indian IT stocks bucked the trend and rallied, helping push the Nifty past 22,500 on strong quarterly earnings. It's a sign that AI valuation anxiety is spreading beyond Silicon Valley, even as the infrastructure buildout accelerates. Closer to home for cost pressures, Zoho's founder Sridhar Vembu announced there will be no salary hikes for employees this year, directly citing rising AI infrastructure costs, and the company is also cutting marketing spend. It's a concrete example of how the capital intensity of AI is squeezing even profitable software companies.

On the application side, there's a cluster of stories about AI reshaping everyday institutions. In India, Infosys co-founder Nandan Nilekani made a strong push at the Bloomberg New Economy Forum for AI-generated exam question papers created on the day of the test, plus a nationwide shift to computer-based testing, arguing this is a solvable fix for the exam leak scandals that have plagued the country. Separately, Kakao in South Korea unveiled "Kanana Space," an AI assistant built into KakaoTalk designed to manage messages, scheduling, and shopping, pushing the messaging app toward becoming a full AI interface. And in Hollywood, there's a more human-centered AI story, Sisi Cao's Hollywood Legacy Gala is spotlighting creativity and human connection even as AI reshapes entertainment, alongside FaceBank Global's work on digital identity and trust, essentially the entertainment industry grappling with how to protect authenticity in an AI-saturated world.
